# Tuza’s conjecture for triangle packing number at most six

**Theorem.** Every finite simple graph $G$ with triangle packing number at most six satisfies $\tau(G)\leq2\nu(G)$.

Here $\nu(G)$ is the maximum number of edge-disjoint triangles and $\tau(G)$ is the minimum number of edges meeting every triangle. The argument uses Kandasamy’s theorem for maximum degree at most ten, the Tutte–Berge formula, the Gallai–Edmonds decomposition, and local link reductions. [Maximum-degree theorem](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15269) · [Matching theory, Schrijver §24](https://www.lamsade.dauphine.fr/~cornaz/Enseignement/M2_MODO/DATA/A3.PDF) · [Packing-five proof](PACKING-FIVE.md)

## Links in a smallest counterexample

Suppose the conclusion fails and choose a counterexample $G$ with the fewest edges, breaking ties by the fewest vertices. All smaller reduction targets below have packing number at most six. Every edge lies in at least two triangles. An edge lying in no triangle could be deleted, and the three edges of a unique triangle containing an edge could be deleted at a cost of one packed triangle and at most two covering edges. The link $L_v=G[N(v)]$ of each vertex consequently has minimum degree at least two. A nonempty matching $M$ inside a set $W$ of link vertices and a set $Q$ meeting every other link edge incident to $W$, with $|Q|\leq|M|$, give a reduction. Delete the spokes from $v$ to $W$ and the edges of $M$. Reinsert the $|M|$ triangles and cover the lost triangles with $M$ and the spokes to $Q$. Thus the link reduction from the packing-five proof applies here as well.

A graph of minimum degree two on at least four vertices has a matching of size two. Indeed, if all edges intersect pairwise, they form a star or triangle. A link on three vertices reduces with one of its triangle edges as $M$ and its opposite vertex as $Q$. On four vertices choose a matching of size two and take the endpoints of one matching edge as $Q$. These two cases force $\delta(G)\geq5$.

A link on five vertices is $K_5$ with at most a matching deleted. If some vertex $z$ has two nonneighbors $p,q$, take its other two vertices as $Q$. When $pq$ is absent, any matching of size two gives a reduction. When $pq$ is present, use that edge and an edge from $z$ to $Q$.

For a link on six vertices, first suppose there is no perfect matching. Tutte's theorem gives a separator whose odd-component count exceeds its size by at least two, by parity. A separator of size zero leaves two triangles, which reduce. A separator of size one cannot leave three odd components because minimum degree two rules out isolates. A separator of size two leaves four isolates, all adjacent to both separator vertices. Take those two vertices as $Q$ and a matching of size two between them and the isolates. Larger separators cannot leave enough odd components.

Now suppose the six-vertex link has a perfect matching. If a vertex $z$ has degree two, let $P$ be its three nonneighbors. If $P$ has a nonedge, its ends and $z$ form an independent triple. Its complement serves as $Q$ with any perfect matching. If $P$ is a clique, every perfect matching contains some edge $pq$ of $P$, since $z$ is matched outside $P$. Take the complement of $\{z,p,q\}$ as $Q$. If instead the link has minimum degree at least three and $z$ has two nonneighbors $p,q$, then $z$ has degree three. Take the complement of $\{z,p,q\}$ as $Q$ if $pq$ is absent or belongs to a perfect matching, using the latter matching when needed. Otherwise the four vertices left after deleting $p,q$ contain a universal vertex $z$ and have no perfect matching. The other three vertices are independent and their complement serves as $Q$ with any perfect matching. Thus any missing edges in the six-vertex link form a matching.

Every link therefore has at least seven edges, since any link of larger order has minimum degree at least two. Each link retains a matching of size two after deleting any two edges. For orders five and six the residual graph has too many edges for a star or triangle. For order at least seven a triangle has too few edges, and a star would require at least six restored incidences away from its center to regain minimum degree two. Two deleted edges supply at most four.

In addition, deleting any matching $M$ from a link leaves a graph with vertex cover number at least three. Otherwise a set $Q$ of size at most two would cover $L_v-M$. If $|M|\geq2$ then $M,Q$ give a whole-link reduction. If $M$ leaves all edges of $L_v$ covered by $Q$, minimum degree two implies $|Q|=2$ and every vertex outside $Q$ adjacent to both members of $Q$. There is then a matching of size two and $Q$ again gives a reduction. The remaining case is a single matching edge with both endpoints outside $Q$. As $|L_v|\geq5$, choose another vertex outside $Q$ and outside that edge. Its edge to $Q$ and the removed edge form a matching of size two giving the same reduction. In particular $L_v-M$ has a matching of size two, since pairwise-intersecting edges in a simple graph form a star or triangle and have vertex cover number at most two.

## Matching deficiency at a maximum-degree vertex

Let $a$ have degree $d=\Delta(G)\geq11$, let $H=G[N(a)]$ and let $X$ consist of the remaining vertices. The minimum degree of $H$ is at least two by the condition on triangles through each spoke. When $X$ is empty it is at least four by $\delta(G)\geq5$.

If the matching number of $H$ is at most $m$, the Tutte–Berge formula gives $S\subseteq V(H)$ with $q\geq s+d-2m$ odd components in $H-S$, where $s=|S|\leq m$. If $i$ of these components are isolated vertices, then

$$
d-s\geq i+3(q-i),\qquad i\geq2s+d-3m.
$$

Every odd component has order at least the smallest positive odd integer at least $\delta(H)-s+1$.

The isolated vertices are pairwise nonadjacent. Each has all its neighbors in $\{a\}\cup S\cup X$. At the first two isolated vertices choose two triangles each, using two-edge link matchings and avoiding the already used link edges. At each further isolated vertex choose one triangle using an unused link edge. The links have at least seven edges, so up to five isolated vertices supply respectively five, six, or seven triangles when there are three, four, or five of them. A matching in the nonisolated part of $H-S$ supplies additional triangles through $a$, whose edges miss every triangle chosen at the isolates.

## The apex must be universal

Suppose $X\ne\varnothing$. A matching of size five in $H$ would give five triangles through $a$. If $X$ is independent, use a matching of size two in the link of any $x\in X$ after deleting those five matching edges. This adds two triangles through $x$. If $xy$ is an edge in $X$, it lies in a triangle $xyz$. The link of $x$ after deleting the matching has an edge avoiding $y,z$, by the vertex-cover bound above. Its triangle through $x$ is disjoint in edges from $xyz$ and all the apex triangles. Both cases give seven triangles, so the matching number of $H$ is at most four.

Now $q\geq s+d-8$ and $i\geq2s+d-12$. If $s\geq3$ there are at least five isolates. If $s=2$ and $d\geq13$ the same holds. For $s=2$ and $d\in\{11,12\}$, four isolates and a remaining edge suffice, and five isolates suffice on their own. In the last case of exactly three isolates, $d=11$ and the bound $q\geq5$ leaves two nontrivial odd components on the other six vertices. Choose one edge from each to extend the five triangles at the isolates. If $s=1$, there are no isolates and $3(d-7)>d-1$, which is impossible.

If $s=0$, all components have order at least three and $3(d-8)\leq d$, so $d\in\{11,12\}$. There are at least two three-vertex components and a third component with an edge. Each three-vertex component is a triangle. Each edge of that triangle has a list of common neighbors of its endpoints in $\{a\}\cup X$, and all three lists contain $a$. If two lists equal $\{a\}$, say for $uv,uw$, then $\{a,v,w\}$ is a triangle component of $L_u$, giving a reduction with that triangle as $W$. If exactly one list is a singleton and the list union has size two, the other two lists contain some $x\in X$, which is then adjacent to all three vertices and belongs to all three lists. If all lists are the same pair $\{a,x\}$, take $W=\{a,v,w,x\}$, $M=\{av,wx\}$, and $Q=\{w,x\}$ in $L_u$. The only edges from $W$ to the rest of the link can be incident to $x$, so this is also a reduction. These cases exhaust the failures of Hall's condition. Choose distinct representatives of the three lists and extend the three edges to triangles. The two components give six edge-disjoint triangles, even if some of their chosen vertices coincide. Any edge of the third component extends with $a$ to a seventh triangle. Thus $X$ must be empty.

## A universal apex of degree at least twelve

The graph $H$ is connected. Otherwise the cones over its components partition all triangles and have disjoint edge sets. Each is smaller than $G$ and satisfies the inequality by minimality. Summing their inequalities proves it for $G$.

For $d\geq12$, a size-seven matching in $H$ would give seven triangles. A matching number at most five is also impossible. Indeed, take $m=5$ in the deficiency bound. If $s\geq4$ it gives at least five isolates. If $s=1,2,3$ there are no isolates, and the lower bounds on odd component orders are respectively $5,3,3$. Multiplying by $s+d-10$ exceeds $d-s$ in each case for $d\geq12$. At $s=0$ there would be at least two components, contradicting connectivity.

Fix a size-six matching $M$ in $H$. The graph $H-M$ is triangle-free because a triangle there would extend the six apex triangles. If some vertex has degree four or five in $H$, its link in $G$ has order five or six. With the apex removed that link still contains the vertex’s neighbors in $H$, inducing a complete graph with at most a matching removed. Thus the vertex and four of its neighbors induce a five-vertex complete graph with at most a matching removed. Deleting $M$ on those five vertices leaves a complement that is the union of two matchings and hence bipartite. An independent set of order three in the complement gives a triangle in $H-M$, a contradiction. It follows that $\delta(H)\geq6$ and $\delta(H-M)\geq5$.

If $d=12$, $H-M$ is bipartite. In a triangle-free graph on $n$ vertices with a shortest odd cycle of length $\ell$, the cycle is chordless and every vertex outside it has at most two neighbors on it. Three or more such neighbors would leave an odd gap that makes a shorter odd cycle with the outside vertex. Hence the degree sum on the cycle is at most $2n$. For $n=12$ and minimum degree five, it would be at least $5\ell\geq25>24$. Use the edges of $M$ and the spokes to the smaller bipartition side of $H-M$ to cover all triangles with at most twelve edges.

Suppose instead $d\geq13$. Apply the deficiency bound with $m=6$. At $s=6$ it gives at least seven isolates. For $1\leq s\leq5$ there are no isolates since $\delta(H)\geq6$. The minimum odd orders are $7,5,5,3,3$ respectively, whose products with $s+d-12$ exceed $d-s$ already at $d=13$. These gaps all increase with $d$. Thus $s=0$, and since $H$ is connected, $q\geq d-12$ implies $d=13$.

For any triangle $T$ in $H$, let $R=H-E(T)$. Its minimum degree is at least four, and only the three vertices of $T$ can have degree below six. If its matching number is at most five, the deficiency bound gives $s\leq5$, $q\geq s+3$ and $i\geq2s-2$. For $s=0,1,2,3$ there are no isolates and the minimum odd orders $5,5,3,3$ give lower bounds of $15,20,15,18$ vertices respectively. Each exceeds $13-s$. For $s=4,5$, there must be at least six or eight isolates respectively, though only the three triangle vertices could be isolated. So $R$ has a matching of size six, which extends using $T$ to a packing of size seven in $G$. Therefore $H$ is triangle-free. With minimum degree six on thirteen vertices it is bipartite by the same shortest-odd-cycle bound. The spokes to a smaller side cover every triangle using at most six edges. In both bipartite cases $\nu(G)=6$ because the matching gave six apex triangles. These covers contradict the choice of $G$.

## A universal apex of degree eleven

The connected graph $H$ has eleven vertices and minimum degree at least four. If its matching number is at most four, the deficiency bound gives $q\geq s+3$ and $i\geq2s-1$, with $s\leq4$. At $s=0$ it disconnects $H$. At $s=1$, there are at least four odd components of order at least five. At $s=2,3$, the forced isolates violate the minimum degree. At $s=4$ there are seven isolates, which give seven triangles by the packing above. Thus $H$ has matching number five.

Call a vertex exposable if some matching of size five in $H$ misses it. Every exposable vertex has degree at least six. To prove this, let $P$ be a matching of size five missing an exposable vertex $v$. If $d_H(v)=4$, its four neighbors in $H$ induce $K_4$ with at most a matching deleted by the five-vertex link restriction in $G$. There are at least two perfect matchings on these neighbors. An edge of either perfect matching meets every edge of the other, so $P$ meets at most one of the perfect matchings in an edge. Use the other perfect matching to make two edge-disjoint triangles through $v$ in $H$. Both avoid $P$ because $P$ also misses $v$. If $d_H(v)=5$, the five neighbors induce $K_5$ with at most a matching deleted by the six-vertex link restriction. After deleting the edges of $P$ this graph has minimum degree at least two and so has a matching of size two. Again it gives two triangles through $v$ avoiding $P$. In either case they extend the five apex triangles from $P$ to seven.

Let $D$ be the set of exposable vertices and $A=N_H(D)\setminus D$. The Gallai–Edmonds decomposition says each component of $H[D]$ is factor-critical and hence odd, and there are $|A|+1$ such components because the matching deficiency of $H$ is one. Put $s=|A|\leq5$. Every vertex of $D$ has degree at least six, all within its component or into $A$. Thus for $s=1,2,3,4,5$ the $s+1$ odd components would require at least $14,15,20,15,18$ vertices respectively. Each bound exceeds $11-s$. Therefore $A=\varnothing$. The set $D$ is nonempty and $H$ is connected, so $D=V(H)$ and $\delta(H)\geq6$.

The graph $H$ has at least 33 edges. If it had no two edge-disjoint triangles, minimality of $G$ would give it a triangle cover with at most two edges. Removing these edges would leave a triangle-free graph on eleven vertices with at least 31 edges, contrary to Mantel’s bound of 30. Choose two edge-disjoint triangles in $H$, and remove their edges to get $R$. All vertices have degree at least four in $R$ except possibly the one vertex shared by the triangles, which has degree at least two. Every vertex off the two triangles retains degree at least six.

If $R$ has matching number at most four, the deficiency bound gives $q\geq s+3$, with $s\leq4$. For $s=0,1$, there are no isolates, and every odd component has order at least five because it contains a vertex of degree at least four in $R$. This requires at least $15$ or $20$ vertices. For $s=2,3$, at most one isolated vertex is possible, giving at least $1+3(q-1)>11-s$ vertices. For $s=4$, all seven remaining vertices must be isolated, although only the at most six vertices of the triangles can become isolated. Thus $R$ has a matching of size five. The associated five apex triangles and the two removed triangles form a packing of size seven in $G$. This final contradiction proves the theorem.
