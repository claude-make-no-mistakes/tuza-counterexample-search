# Tuza’s inequality for triangle packing number at most seven

**Theorem.** Let $G$ be a finite simple graph. Write $\nu(G)$ for the maximum number of edge-disjoint triangles and $\tau(G)$ for the minimum number of edges meeting every triangle. If $\nu(G)\leq7$, then $\tau(G)\leq2\nu(G)$.

The argument uses the maximum-degree-ten theorem, Puleo's reducible sets, and the Tutte–Berge and Gallai–Edmonds matching theorems. [Kandasamy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15269) · [Puleo](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1308.2211) · [Schrijver, Section 24](https://www.lamsade.dauphine.fr/~cornaz/Enseignement/M2_MODO/DATA/A3.PDF)

## Local reductions and packings at isolates

Suppose the theorem fails. Choose a counterexample $G$ minimizing its number of edges and then its number of vertices. Every proper subgraph satisfies the inequality. The packing-six theorem gives $\nu(G)=7$, so a cover of size at most fourteen suffices. An edge in no triangle could be deleted. If an edge lies in one triangle, removing all three edges of that triangle reduces the packing by at least one and the covering problem by at most two. Thus every edge lies in at least two triangles and each link $L_v=G[N(v)]$ has minimum degree at least two. [Packing-six theorem](PACKING-SIX.md)

In a link, a nonempty matching $M$ in a vertex set $W$, together with a set $Q$ of at most $|M|$ vertices covering every other link edge incident with $W$, gives a reduction. Delete the spokes from the apex to $W$ and the edges of $M$. The triangles of $M$ extend any residual packing. The edges of $M$ and the spokes to $Q$ extend a residual cover. In particular $M$ and a vertex cover of $L_v-M$ of size at most $|M|$ give a reduction. A component of order three is a triangle and reduces using one edge and its opposite vertex. A component of order four has a two-edge matching and reduces using that matching and the ends of one of its edges. Thus $G$ is robust in Puleo's sense, with each link component of order at least five. It has no reducible set as in Puleo's Definition 2.1, since every proper subgraph satisfies the inequality. In particular $\delta(G)\geq5$, the vertices of degree at most six are independent, each of their links has complement of maximum degree one and with an edge count different from two, and any vertex subsuming a degree-five vertex has degree at least nine. These last three facts are Puleo's Lemma 2.7(a), (b), and (e). A vertex subsumes another if its closed neighborhood contains the other's. [Puleo, Lemmas 2.6 and 2.7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1308.2211)

Each link has at least eight edges. This follows from the near-complete links of orders five and six, and from minimum degree two for order at least eight. At order seven, equality with seven edges would mean every component is a cycle. A maximum alternating matching on a cycle leaves a graph covered by at most that many vertices, giving a reduction. To see the vertex-cover bound, an even cycle leaves a matching, and an odd cycle leaves a two-edge path and isolated edges.

Deleting any matching $M$ from a link leaves vertex-cover number at least three. If $Q$ of size at most two covers the residual and $|M|\geq2$, then $M,Q$ give a reduction. If at most one matching edge was deleted and $Q$ covers the original link, minimum degree two forces every vertex outside $Q$ to see both members of $Q$. There is then a two-edge matching and a reduction using $Q$. Otherwise there is exactly one deleted edge outside $Q$. Choose a vertex outside $Q$ and that edge, and pair its edge to $Q$ with the deleted edge, again giving a reduction. In particular the residual has a matching of size two, as a family of pairwise-intersecting edges has vertex-cover number at most two.

Every link also retains a matching of size two after deleting the union of two matchings of size two. Intersect the deleted edge set $F$ with the link if some edges were absent. At link order five or six, the link has at least nine or twelve edges respectively, and the residual is too large to be a star or triangle. At order at least seven the residual has at least four edges, so if it had no two-edge matching it would be a star with center $c$. Minimum degree two implies each of the at least six vertices other than $c$ is incident with $F$. If $F$ has a three-edge matching, use it and cover the remaining edges by $c$ and at most one additional vertex, a reduction. Otherwise $F$ consists of two disjoint two-edge paths. Indeed it has at most four edges, is a union of two matchings, and must meet at least six vertices. Its components are paths or even cycles, and every other such edge pattern meeting six vertices contains a three-edge matching. Here $c$ is outside both paths. All four path ends see $c$ by minimum degree two. Select one edge per path and an edge from $c$ to an unused path end. The remaining edges are covered by $c$ and one end of each unused path edge, again a reduction. The argument includes deletion of just one two-edge matching.

Let $a$ have maximum degree $d\geq11$, using Kandasamy's theorem. Set $H=G[N(a)]$ and let $X$ be the nonneighbors of $a$ other than $a$. Write $\mu$ for graph matching number. The link $H$ has minimum degree at least two. If $\mu(H)\leq m$, Tutte–Berge supplies $S\subseteq V(H)$, with $s=|S|\leq m$, such that $H-S$ has $q\geq s+d-2m$ odd components. If $i$ of them are isolated vertices, then

$$d-s\geq i+3(q-i),\qquad i\geq2s+d-3m.$$

An odd component has order at least the smallest positive odd integer at least $\delta(H)-s+1$. The same bounds hold for another graph of order $d$ in place of $H$.

Isolates in $H-S$ are independent and all their neighbors belong to $\{a\}\cup S\cup X$. At up to three isolates take two triangles each using two-edge link matchings and avoiding the previous base edges. At each additional isolate take a single triangle with a fresh base edge. Links have at least eight edges. Two, three, four, or five isolates supply respectively four, six, seven, or eight triangles. An unused matching in the nonisolated part of $H-S$ can add triangles through $a$ because its endpoints are outside all links and spokes used at the isolates.

## The maximum-degree vertex is universal

Suppose $X$ is nonempty. Any six-edge matching in $H$ gives six triangles through $a$. If $X$ is independent, the residual link of any $x\in X$ after deleting the matching gives two triangles through $x$. Otherwise start with a triangle $xyz$ containing an edge $xy$ of $X$. The residual link of $x$ has an edge avoiding $y,z$, since it has vertex-cover number at least three. Its triangle and $xyz$ add two. Hence $\mu(H)\leq5$.

If $\mu(H)\leq4$, use the Tutte–Berge bounds with $m=4$. At $s\geq3$ there are at least five isolates. At $s=2$ there are at least three isolates. Four isolates and an edge elsewhere suffice, and if exactly three remain then $d=11$, $q\geq5$, and the other six vertices form at least two nontrivial odd components. Take an edge in each. At $s=1$ there are no isolates and $3(d-7)>d-1$. At $s=0$ minimum degree two gives $3(d-8)\leq d$, so $d=11$ or $12$. The components have orders $3,3,5$ or $3,3,3,3$, respectively. In either case two of the three-vertex components can each furnish three triangles, using their edges and distinct third vertices from $\{a\}\cup X$. To verify this, give each edge of the component its list of common neighbors in $\{a\}\cup X$. All lists contain $a$. If two lists equal $\{a\}$, say for $uv,uw$, the triangle on $a,v,w$ is a component of $L_u$ and reduces. If one list is a singleton and the union has size two, the other two lists contain an $x$ adjacent to all three vertices, so it must belong to the singleton as well. If all three lists are the same pair $\{a,x\}$, use the link reduction at $u$ with $W=\{a,v,w,x\}$, $M=\{av,wx\}$ and $Q=\{w,x\}$. Only $x$ can have a link edge going outside $W$. These are all possible failures of Hall's condition. Use distinct representatives for the three edges. In the remaining components a matching of size two yields two more triangles through $a$. This rules out $\mu(H)\leq4$.

Fix a five-edge matching $M$ in $H$. The set $X$ is a clique. If two of its vertices $x,y$ were nonadjacent, two new triangles through $x$ could avoid $M$. They can obstruct triangles through $y$ only by their two base edges, since neither triangle contains $y$. The link $L_y-M$ has at least three edges, so it gives a third new triangle.

The clique $X$ has at most two vertices. If it has at least six, take three edge-disjoint triangles inside it. If it has five, there is an edge $xh$ between $X$ and $H$ by minimum degree five. This edge lies in at least two triangles, at most one of which has its other edge incident with $h$ in $M$. Choose a triangle avoiding $M$, and two edge-disjoint triangles in $X$ avoiding its at most one edge there. Such two triangles exist in $K_5$ minus an edge, for instance $012$ and $034$ if the missing edge is $13$.

If $|X|=4$, choose $x\in X$ and let $T=X-\{x\}$ be a triangle. The link $L_x$ has at least two vertices in $H$, all of which retain positive degree in $R=L_x-M-E(T)$ since they lose at most one incident edge. We claim $R$ has a two-edge matching. Otherwise its edges form a star or a triangle. For a star with center $c\in T$, the edges of $L_x-M$ are covered by $c$ and one more vertex of $T$, a contradiction. If $c\in H$, choose a vertex $h\ne c$ in $N(x)\cap H$. Minimum degree two in $L_x$ forces a deleted matching edge incident with $h$. The matching $M\cap E(L_x)$ together with one edge of $T$ gives a reduction, using $c$ and the opposite vertex of $T$ as a cover. In the triangle case all vertices of $N(x)\cap H$ belong to the triangle. If there are three of them, the original link consists of two triangles because all potential matching edges among those three are already present in $R$. If there are two, the link consists of two triangles meeting at one vertex for the same reason. Both violate the link restrictions already proved. This proves the claim. The two new triangles through $x$ and $T$ give three beyond the five apex triangles.

If $|X|=3$, write $X=\{x,y,z\}$. For each $x\in X$, there is an edge of $H[N(x)]$ outside $M$ because the other two vertices of $X$ do not cover $L_x-M$. If the union of these three edge sets has at least two distinct edges, choose distinct edges at distinct vertices of $X$. They give two triangles through $X$ alongside the triangle on $X$. Otherwise the three sets equal a singleton $\{uv\}$. Each $x\in X$ sees both $u,v$ and some other $h\in H$. In $L_x-M$ this $h$ has positive degree but lies on no remaining edge with both ends in $H$. Thus $h$ sees another vertex of $X$, say $y$. Use the triangles $xuv,xyh,yzu$. All three avoid $M$ and are edge-disjoint. This finishes the bound $|X|\leq2$.

We have $\delta(H)\geq3$. A degree-two vertex in $H$ would need both vertices of $X$ as neighbors to reach degree five in $G$, leaving $a$ with two missing neighbors in its five-vertex link. Furthermore, a degree-three vertex in $H$ has exactly one neighbor $x$ in $X$. With two it would violate the six-vertex link restriction. With this unique $x$, the missing edge $ax$ in the five-vertex link forces $x$ adjacent to all three of its $H$-neighbors.

Every vertex $v$ missed by a maximum matching $M$ of $H$ is adjacent to every $x\in X$. Suppose $xv$ is absent. The graph $L_v-\{a\}-M$ has vertex-cover number at least two, hence at least two edges. A matching of size two in $L_x-M$ gives two triangles through $x$. Its two base edges can exhaust the available edges at $v$ only if there are exactly two and both were used. In that case $L_x-M$ has a vertex outside those four endpoints and of positive degree, because the link has order at least five and minimum degree at least two before deleting $M$. Use one edge incident with that vertex and whichever of the former two edges is disjoint from it. This leaves an unused base at $v$. The three triangles so chosen avoid the five apex triangles, since $v$ is unmatched and neither $x$ nor $v$ uses $a$ in its new triangles. Thus $xv$ must be present.

Use Gallai–Edmonds on $H$. Let $D$ be the vertices missed by some maximum matching and take $S=N_H(D)\setminus D$. There are $q=s+d-10$ odd factor-critical components in $H[D]$. The other components of $H-S$ are even and have perfect matchings. A maximum matching within $H-S$ has $5-s$ edges. Every vertex of an odd component sees every vertex of $X$ by the preceding observation. If $i\geq5$ then isolates give eight triangles. If $i=3$ or $4$, the bound $i\geq2s+d-15$ gives respectively $s\leq3$ or $s\leq4$, so the isolate pack and $5-s$ apex triangles in the other components suffice.

For $i\leq2$ and $s\geq3$, the only possibility is $s=3$, $d=11$, $i=2$. The other two odd components are triangles. Each gives two triangles by using different edges extended through $a$ and a fixed $x\in X$. The two isolates give four more. For $s=2$ there are no isolates, and $3(d-8)\leq d-2$ forces $d=11$ and three triangular components. In each, the three vertices see both $a,x$ and each has a neighbor in $S$. Some pair shares a neighbor in $S$ because $|S|=2$. Use it as a third vertex for that pair and use $a,x$ for the other two pairs. This gives nine triangles.

For $s=1$ there are no isolates, and $3(d-9)\leq d-1$ forces $11\leq d\leq13$. An odd component of order three is a triangle complete to the sole vertex of $S$ by $\delta(H)\geq3$. It yields three triangles using that vertex, $a$, and a fixed $x\in X$. In an odd component $B$ of order five or seven, use a near-perfect matching at $a$ and a disjoint two-edge matching at $x$. Such matchings exist because $\delta(B)\geq2$. If the residual of a near-perfect matching has no two-edge matching, its minimum degree at least one forces a spanning star and the deleted matching pairs all leaves. Change the first matching to a spoke on the first leaf pair and all the other leaf-pair edges. The second matching uses the first pair edge and a spoke to a different pair. For $d=11$ the component orders are $3,7$ or $5,5$ or $3,3,4$, where the even component supplies a two-edge matching through $a$. For $d=12$ the orders are $3,3,5$, and for $d=13$ there are four triples. An even component of order two is impossible since each of its vertices would have degree at most two in $H$. Every listed case gives at least eight triangles.

For $s=0$, all odd components have order at least five, so $5(d-10)\leq d$ gives $d=11$ or $12$. An order-five component induces $K_5$ minus at most a matching since its minimum degree is at least three. It supplies five triangles using $a,x$. Label it $0,1,2,3,4$ with missing edges among $01,23$. Use $024$, the matching $03,14$ at $a$, and the matching $12,34$ at $x$. An order-seven or order-eleven odd component supports a near-perfect matching at $a$ followed by a three-edge matching at $x$. To check this, remove the near-perfect matching. Minimum degree is still at least two. For order $b\in\{7,11\}$, if the new matching number were at most two, a Tutte–Berge separator of order zero or one would need more vertices than available since the odd components have order at least three. A separator of order two leaves $b-2$ isolates, each adjacent to both separator vertices. Each isolate had degree at least three, so the removed matching paired all $b-2$ isolates with one another, impossible at odd order. For $d=12$ the odd components have orders five and seven. For $d=11$ the component orders are $5,6$ or $7,4$ or $11$. An even component supports its perfect matching at $a$, and the other components supply the triangles just described. All the constructions in different components use disjoint edges. This excludes the nonuniversal case.

## A universal vertex of degree at least thirteen

Now $H$ has minimum degree at least four and is connected. If it were disconnected, the cones on its components would satisfy the inequality by minimality. Their edge sets are disjoint and every triangle lies in one cone, so the inequalities add. Since $a$ is universal, $\mu(H)\leq7$.

First $\mu(H)\geq6$ if $d\geq12$. For $m=5$ and $s=1,2,3$, the minimum odd component orders are $5,3,3$ and their products with $s+d-10$ exceed $d-s$. Connectivity rules out $s=0$. At $s\geq4$ there are at least five isolates, a contradiction.

Suppose $d\geq13$ and $\mu(H)=6$. A vertex missed by a six-edge matching has degree at least six in $H$. A degree-four vertex has a four-vertex neighborhood inducing $K_4$ minus at most a matching. There are at least two perfect matchings on these four vertices and any edge of one meets every edge of the other. Thus one of them avoids the given matching, and gives two triangles at the missed vertex. For degree five, the corresponding five-vertex neighborhood is $K_5$ minus at most a matching; deleting the given matching leaves minimum degree two and hence a two-edge matching. In both cases the two new triangles add to the six apex triangles.

Use the Gallai–Edmonds decomposition with $D$ the vertices missed by some maximum matching, $S=N_H(D)\setminus D$ and $s=|S|$. There are $q=s+d-12$ odd components of $H[D]$. Their vertices have degree at least six and only $S$ has neighbors into $D$ from outside it. At $s=1,2,3,4,5$ the minimum odd orders are $7,5,5,3,3$. Their products with $s+d-12$ exceed $d-s$ at $d=13$ and at every larger $d$. At $s=6$ all $d-6$ remaining vertices are isolated, giving eight triangles. Thus $s=0$, and since $D$ is nonempty and $H$ connected, $D=V(H)$ and $q=1$. This gives $d=13$ and $\delta(H)\geq6$.

If $H$ has at most one edge-disjoint triangle, a cover of $H$ of size at most two exists by minimality. Together with twelve spokes it gives a cover of $G$ of size at most fourteen. Otherwise remove the edges of two edge-disjoint triangles to obtain $R$. Except for a possible shared vertex of degree at least two, $R$ has minimum degree at least four. Vertices outside those triangles still have degree at least six. If $\mu(R)\leq5$, then $q\geq s+3$ and $i\geq2s-2$. At $s=0,1$ there are no isolates, and any order-three component would contain a vertex of degree at least four in $R$, impossible. The odd orders are at least five and exceed the available total. At $s=2,3$ there is at most one isolate and $1+3(q-1)>13-s$. At $s=5$ eight required isolates exceed the at most six vertices on the two triangles. At $s=4$ there are at least six isolates, so the two triangles are disjoint and all six of their vertices are isolated in $R-S$. They each see all four vertices of $S$ in $H$, by the minimum degree six. The other three vertices outside $S$ retain degree at least six and are nonadjacent to the isolates in $R$, so they form a triangle and also see all of $S$. These three triangles in $H-S$ extend to nine edge-disjoint triangles using distinct vertices of $S$ for the three edges of each. If there is no such separator then $R$ has a six-edge matching, which with the two removed triangles gives eight in $G$. This excludes $\mu(H)=6$ at $d\geq13$.

It remains that $\mu(H)=7$. If $M$ is a seven-edge matching, $H-M$ must be triangle-free. A vertex of degree four or five in $H$ would give an induced $K_5$ minus at most a matching, by the small-link restrictions. Inside those five vertices the complement after removing $M$ is a union of two matchings, and so bipartite. An independent triple of that complement gives a triangle in $H-M$. Hence $\delta(H)\geq6$.

The deficiency bounds with $m=7$ exclude $d\geq16$. At $s\geq6$ there are at least seven isolates. At $s=1,2,3,4,5$ the minimum odd orders $7,5,5,3,3$ times $s+d-14$ exceed $d-s$ when $d\geq16$, and at $s=0$ connectivity rules out $q\geq2$. Thus $d=14$ or $15$.

If there is a triangle $T$ in $H$, put $R=H-E(T)$. If its matching number is at most six, then $q\geq s+d-12$ and $i\geq2s+d-18$ with $s\leq6$. Only the three vertices of $T$ have degree possibly below six, and none has degree below four. At $s=0$ each odd component has order at least seven, since an order-five component would contain an unaffected vertex of degree at least six. This excludes $d=15$. For $d=14$ it means there are two order-seven components. The triangle crosses between them because $H$ is connected. At least five vertices in each component lie outside $T$, and their degree at least six makes them complete to their component. This gives a $K_5$ in each. The cones over the two cliques each pack four triangles. Indeed a cone over $K_5$ contains an octahedron $K_{2,2,2}$, whose edges partition into four triangles by choosing one vertex in each pair and taking the choices with even parity.

For $s=1$ the minimum odd order five and for $s=3$ the minimum odd order three give too many vertices. For $s=2$ and $d=15$ the minimum odd order three also gives too many. For $s=2$ and $d=14$ four order-three components would be necessary, though only one could avoid every unaffected vertex, and those vertices force order at least five. For $s=4,5$ the bound on $i$ exceeds three, impossible. At $s=6$ all $d-6$ remaining vertices are isolated in $R-S$, and at least $d-9\geq5$ are unchanged in $H-S$. They yield eight triangles at isolates. In every remaining case $R$ has a seven-edge matching, which adds seven apex triangles to $T$. Therefore $H$ is triangle-free, and at most $d-1\leq14$ spokes cover all triangles. All order-at-least-fourteen cones are excluded.

## A universal vertex of degree twelve

The matching number of $H$ is six by the preceding bound for $d\geq12$, so it has a perfect matching. If $v$ has degree five in $H$, its closed neighborhood induces $K_6$ minus a matching. After deleting any perfect matching of $H$, the complement in these six vertices is a union of two matchings. This is a union of paths and even cycles, with the two parts of each component differing by at most one vertex. Its components therefore have a bipartition with both parts of size three. The two parts give two triangles in $H$ avoiding the perfect matching. Together with six apex triangles this is impossible. Similarly if a degree-four vertex has its closed neighborhood inducing $K_5$, removal of a matching still leaves two triangles, by extending two disjoint edges through a vertex missed by the matching on these five vertices. Thus a remaining degree-four vertex $v$ has four neighbors inducing $K_4$ minus one edge by Puleo's link restriction. Denote its neighbors by $p,q,x,y$, with $pq$ missing. The vertices $x,y$ subsume $v$ in $G$ and so have degree at least eight in $H$. The vertices $p,q$ have degree at least six in $H$ since the vertices of degree at most five in $H$ are independent. Write $O=V(H)\setminus\{v,p,q,x,y\}$, with $|O|=7$. Then $x,y$ each see at least four vertices of $O$, and $p,q$ each see at least three.

Delete the five edges among $p,q,x,y$ to obtain $R$. A perfect matching of $R$ uses only one edge inside the closed neighborhood of $v$, joining $v$ to one of its neighbors. Deleting this matching from $H$ therefore leaves a $K_5$ minus at most two edges on the closed neighborhood of $v$, which has two edge-disjoint triangles. For two disjoint missing edges, use the remaining vertex and a matching on the other four. For missing edges $01,02$, take $034$ and $123$. The two triangles give eight alongside the six apex triangles.

If $R$ has no perfect matching, then $q\geq s+2$, $i\geq2s-3$, and $s\leq5$. At $s=0$, the graph $R$ is connected since each removed edge has its ends joined by a path through $v$. At $s=1$, if $v$ remains, its component contains at least three of its neighbors, and every other component contains a vertex of $O$ of degree at least four in $R$. If $v$ is removed, each remaining component contains such a vertex since each of $p,q,x,y$ has a neighbor in $O$. Thus the odd components have order at least five, too large for $q\geq3$. At $s=2,3$ there are no isolates. The vertices of $O$ retain degree at least four. If $v$ remains, it and its remaining neighbors have positive degree, and if $v$ is removed there are at most two other separator vertices to remove from their outside neighbors. The bound of three per odd component exceeds $12-s$.

At $s=4$ there must be at least five isolates. If $v$ is removed, only $p,q$ can be isolated. If $v$ remains, only $v$ and vertices of $O$ can be isolated. Then at least four vertices of $O$ are isolated and complete to $S$ by their minimum degree four. Take four such vertices and match them to $S$, using $S$ as the cover in a scoped link reduction at $a$. At $s=5$ all seven remaining vertices are isolated. If $v$ remains, all four of its neighbors are in $S$, leaving six isolated vertices of $O$ with at least four neighbors each in $S$. In general if a set $I$ of at least five isolates each has at least four neighbors in a five-vertex separator, there is a matching from $Q=N_H(I)$ into $I$ saturating $Q$. Each singleton subset of $Q$ has a neighbor, and each larger subset meets every vertex of $I$ since an isolate misses at most one separator vertex. This gives Hall's condition. Using $Q$ as the cover and $Q\cup I$ as the scope gives a reduction. In our case the edges at the isolates of $O$ are the same in $H$ and $R$.

If $s=5$ and $v$ is removed, let $I=O\setminus S$. Every vertex of $I$ sees all four members of $S\setminus\{v\}$, since it has degree at least four and does not see $v$. If $|I|\geq4$ there is again a scoped reduction. Otherwise $|I|=3$ and $S\setminus\{v\}$ consists of four vertices of $O$. All four see both $x,y$, as those two are isolated in $R-S$ but each has at least four neighbors in $O$. The local octahedron on $a,v,p,q,x,y$ that omits $av,xy,pq$ packs four triangles. Add a triangle $xyz$ with a common neighbor $z\in O$, and three apex triangles from a matching between $I$ and $S\setminus\{v\}$. The eight triangles use disjoint edges. This rules out a degree-four vertex and proves $\delta(H)\geq6$.

If $H$ has at most one triangle in a packing, minimality gives a cover of size at most two and eleven spokes cover the apex triangles. Otherwise choose two edge-disjoint triangles and let $R$ be obtained by deleting their edges. As before only a possible shared vertex has degree below four in $R$, and every unaffected vertex has degree at least six. If $R$ has a perfect matching it supplies six apex triangles in addition to the two removed ones. If not, $q\geq s+2$ and $i\geq2s-3$, with $s\leq5$. At $s=0$ an odd component has order at least five, and an order-five component is also impossible. All five vertices would have lost an edge to the other components, while each removed triangle can cross the cut at at most two vertices on that side. Thus at least two odd components of order at least seven would be necessary. At $s=1$ the odd components have order at least five, exceeding the available total. At $s=2$ at most one isolate is possible, so the only component orders that can fit are one isolate and three triples. Each triple would have to consist of affected vertices, which are at most six, not nine. At $s=3$ at most one isolate is possible and the total is again too large. At $s=5$ seven isolates exceed the number of affected vertices.

At $s=4$ there are at least five isolates. If the removed triangles are disjoint, the isolates include a triangle and a disjoint pair forming an edge in $H$. All five selected vertices see all four vertices of $S$ by minimum degree six. If the triangles meet at $v$, all five affected vertices are isolated. Their four other vertices see all of $S$, and $v$ has at least two neighbors in $S$. In either case write $uvw$ for one of the triangles and $xy$ for the other pair, with only $v$ possibly missing some neighbors in $S$. Name its two neighbors $s_1,s_2$ and the others $s_3,s_4$. Extend $vu,vw,uw,xy$ through $s_1,s_2,s_3,s_4$ respectively. Then use the four unused matching edges $s_1w,s_2x,s_3y,s_4u$ for triangles through $a$. This gives eight, excluding the universal degree-twelve case.

## A universal vertex of degree eleven

Suppose first that $H$ has a degree-four vertex $v$ with neighbors $p,q,x,y$, and write $O=V(H)\setminus\{v,p,q,x,y\}$, with $|O|=6$. The graph on the four neighbors is $K_4$ or $K_4$ minus one edge by Puleo. If one edge is missing, name it $pq$. Each vertex universal on the four neighbors subsumes $v$ in $G$, so it has degree at least eight in $H$ and sees at least four vertices of $O$. In particular $x,y$ have at least two common neighbors in $O$. The octahedron on $a,v,p,q,x,y$ omitting $av,xy,pq$ packs four triangles which avoid these three edges.

If the four neighbors form a clique, extend both $xy$ and $pq$ to triangles using common neighbors in $O$, possibly the same vertex. This gives six triangles disjoint in edges from all triangles in the cone over $O$. Thus the matching number of $H[O]$ is at most one. Cover the four spokes from $a$ to the neighbors of $v$ and the six edges among those neighbors. The remaining triangles use an edge of $H[O]$. If there are at most four such edges, use them in the cover. Otherwise their pairwise intersection makes $H[O]$ a five-edge star, say with center $w$. Each remaining triangle uses a spoke to $w$ from $a$ or a neighbor of $v$. If at most two of those neighbors see $w$, cover all remaining triangles with these at most three spokes. Otherwise pick three of them and $a$. Each chosen neighbor of $v$ sees at least three of the five leaves of the star, and $a$ sees them all. Hall's condition gives distinct leaves for these four vertices. Their four triangles using $w$ are edge-disjoint from the original octahedron, giving eight.

Suppose the four neighbors induce $K_4$ minus $pq$. The octahedron and a triangle $xyz$, where $z\in C=N_O(x)\cap N_O(y)$, pack five, edge-disjoint from the cone over $O$. Thus that cone has packing number at most two. Cover the four apex spokes to $p,q,x,y$ and the five edges among them, at cost nine. The remaining triangles either lie in the cone over $O$ or extend an edge in $F=H[O]$ through one of $p,q,x,y$. If $F$ has at most five edges, all of them suffice. Otherwise its matching number is two: three would yield three triangles at $a$, and at most one would make its edges a star or triangle and give at most five edges.

Call an edge of $F$ compulsory if it belongs to every two-edge matching of $F$. An edge $e$ of $F$ extended to a triangle through $p$ or $q$ must be compulsory. Otherwise take the five triangles including $xyz$, the triangle extending $e$, and two apex triangles from a matching avoiding $e$. The same reasoning works for an extension through $x$ or $y$ if $C$ has a vertex outside $e$, on choosing that vertex as $z$. Since $|C|\geq2$, at most one edge is exceptional. There is also at most one compulsory edge. If a two-edge matching were unique, every other edge would have to join its two edges, and at most two such joining edges could exist without two being disjoint. This contradicts $|E(F)|\geq6$.

Suppose $F$ is triangle-free. If it were nonbipartite, it would have a chordless five-cycle on its six vertices. Any edge from the sixth vertex to the cycle would give a three-edge matching, so the graph would have only five edges. Thus $F$ is bipartite. It has a vertex cover of size two by its matching number. Use the two spokes to those vertices to cover apex triangles, and at most two $F$ edges to cover all extensions through $p,q,x,y$. The total is at most fourteen.

If $F$ has a triangle $T$, then $F-E(T)$ has matching number at most one, or $T$ together with two apex triangles gives three in the cone over $O$. There are at least three residual edges, forming a triangle or a star. If they form a triangle, there is no compulsory edge. Indeed two edge-disjoint triangles share at most one vertex. For any edge to avoid, one may choose an edge in its triangle different from it and an edge in the other triangle disjoint from the first, choosing the edge opposite the shared vertex on one side when necessary. If the residual edges form a star with center $c$ outside $T$, there is again no compulsory edge. For a star edge to avoid, use a different star edge and a triangle edge avoiding its leaf. For an edge of $T$ to avoid, use a star edge to a leaf outside $T$ if such a leaf exists. Otherwise all three triangle vertices are leaves and the graph contains a $K_4$, which has three perfect matchings. In these cases the cone over $O$ has a cover of size at most four by minimality and at most one exceptional edge is needed for the other extensions. Finally if $c$ is in $T$, the edge $f$ opposite $c$ is compulsory and all other edges of $F$ are incident to $c$. Cover the remaining triangles by $f$, the spoke $ac$, and at most one exceptional edge. This completes the case of a degree-four vertex.

Now suppose $\delta(H)\geq5$ and $v$ has degree five in $H$. Its five neighbors induce $K_5$ minus at most one edge, since the apex is adjacent to all of them and Puleo forbids a complement with exactly two edges. Let $O$ be the five vertices outside the closed neighborhood of $v$ in $H$. If the neighborhood is complete, it forms a $K_7$ with $a,v$. The seven triples of the Fano plane partition its edges into triangles. Any edge of $O$ adds an eighth triangle at $a$. If $O$ is independent, all five vertices of $O$ see all five neighbors of $v$ by minimum degree five. Match them to these neighbors and use the latter as a cover for a scoped reduction at $a$. If the neighborhood has a missing edge $pq$, label the same $K_7$ decomposition so that one of its triples is $apq$. Omitting this triple leaves six triangles and leaves the spokes $ap,aq$ free. Each of $p,q$ has a neighbor in $O$ by minimum degree five. If they have distinct representatives there, add the corresponding apex triangles. Otherwise both have the same sole neighbor $z\in O$. An edge in $O\setminus\{z\}$ would also give two new apex triangles together with $pz$. If no such edge exists, every vertex in $O\setminus\{z\}$ has degree at most four in $H$, since it sees at most $z$ and the three other neighbors of $v$. This is impossible.

It remains that $\delta(H)\geq6$. If $\nu(H)\leq3$, use a cover of $H$ of size at most six from minimality. Its triangle-free residual has an independent set of size three. A vertex of degree at least three has three independent neighbors, and maximum degree two permits a greedy independent set of size at least $\lceil 11/3\rceil$. The spokes outside the independent set give a total at most fourteen. Otherwise choose three edge-disjoint triangles, and let $R$ be obtained by deleting their edges. A matching of size five in $R$ gives eight triangles in $G$. Suppose no such matching exists. Then Tutte–Berge gives $q\geq s+3$ and $s\leq4$.

Call a vertex shared if it occurs in at least two chosen triangles. There are at most three shared vertices, and only they can have degree below four in $R$. If one occurs in all three triangles, it is the only shared vertex. If there are three shared vertices, each is shared by a distinct pair of triangles, and every edge between shared vertices belongs to a deleted triangle. At $s=0,1$ at most one isolate is possible, namely the vertex shared by all three. An odd component of order three would have to contain three shared vertices, which have no edges between them in $R$. At $s=1$ the remaining odd components of order at least five cannot fit. At $s=0$, at least two vertices are unaffected by the deleted edges and retain degree at least six. The only count that could fit is one isolate plus two order-five components, which cannot contain such a vertex. At $s=2$ the five required odd components can fit only with three isolates and two triples. The isolates must be the three shared vertices, so only six vertices occurred in the three triangles. At least three unaffected vertices remain outside $S$, and their degree at least six prevents them lying in either triple. At $s=3$ there are at most three isolates and six odd components cannot fit in eight vertices.

At $s=4$ all seven remaining vertices are isolated in $R-S$. Their graph in $H$ has minimum degree at least two and contains at least seven edges, all from the chosen triangles. If a triangle has a vertex in $S$, there are at most seven such edges. Equality would give a 2-regular graph containing two triangles on seven vertices, which is impossible. Thus all three triangles lie outside $S$ and their union is the whole graph there. They form either a chain of three triangles with the middle triangle sharing distinct vertices with the two ends, or three triangles with a common center. Ordinary vertices of degree two there see all of $S$, and a shared vertex of degree four sees at least two vertices of $S$.

For the chain, skip the edge joining the two shared vertices. Extend the other eight edges of the chain through vertices of $\{a\}\cup S$. At each shared vertex at least three such vertices are available. Give the edges from the two shared vertices to their common ordinary neighbor different extensions. At each shared vertex independently extend its other two edges through distinct unused available vertices. Each remaining edge between ordinary vertices can use any of the five possible extensions avoiding those already used at its two ends. This gives eight edge-disjoint triangles. For the three triangles with center $v$, let one be $vxy$. If $v$ has no neighbor in $S$, its link in $G$ is a star centered at $a$ with three disjoint leaf-pair edges. These leaf-pair edges and the cover $\{a\}$ give a reduction. Otherwise let $s\in S$ see $v$. Replace $vxy$ by $vxs$ and $vya$. Keep the two other original triangles and use their four ordinary vertices to form four apex triangles, one with each of the four vertices of $S$. This gives eight. The contradiction proves the theorem.
